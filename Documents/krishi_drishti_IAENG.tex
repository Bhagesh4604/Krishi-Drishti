\begin{abstract}
Smallholder farming households cultivating plots under two hectares represent the predominant demographic in developing agrarian economies, yet conventional Measurement, Reporting, and Verification (MRV) systems impose administrative and laboratory costs that render individual carbon credit certification economically impossible. This paper presents the computational architecture and systems implementation of \textbf{Krishi-Drishti}, a mobile-first digital MRV infrastructure designed to automate agricultural carbon credit issuance through distributed cloud-edge coordination. Utilizing Sentinel-2 MSI Bottom-of-Atmosphere surface reflectance tiles via Google Earth Engine, a two-tier stacking ensemble regressor (Random Forest, XGBoost, CatBoost with Ridge meta-learning) predicts topsoil Soil Organic Carbon (SOC) with $R^2 = 0.89$ and RMSE of $1.52\,$g/kg across 50 ground validation benchmarks without requiring on-site diagnostic laboratories. Issuance of tradeable carbon credits is executed via a Four-Pillar readiness gate co-aligned with Verra VM0042 and Indian Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE) criteria, completing verification-to-issuance in under 48 hours. Immutable credit provenance is achieved through sequential SHA-256 state chaining paired with diurnal Merkle Root Anchoring, issuing tamper-proof HarvestTokens directly to farmers at \rupee 1,200/$\text{tCO}_2\text{e}$ without the overhead of public blockchain ledgers. System usability evaluations with rural operators achieved an 84.0/100 SUS index, demonstrating that combining cloud-edge processing with stacked ensemble learning establishes a scalable engineering pathway for democratic environmental carbon credit issuance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{A}{gricultural} decarbonization represents a pivotal frontier in global climate mitigation, yet certifying carbon sequestration on smallholder farms remains bottlenecked by computational, infrastructural, and economic constraints [1]. In emerging agrarian markets, agrarian holdings are fragmented across millions of sub-two-hectare properties [2]. While sustainable management interventions---such as minimal tillage, residue preservation, and agroforestry---measurably augment organic carbon in the soil profile, conventional carbon certification frameworks rely heavily on manual verification workflows. These legacy auditing pipelines require certified inspectors to conduct physical field surveys and commercial laboratory wet-chemical testing, imposing overhead expenses between USD \$1,500 and \$3,000 per property and spanning audit durations of 6 to 18 months [2], [3]. For a marginal cultivator generating modest annual offset volumes, verification expenditures exceed potential market returns by an order of magnitude.

Overcoming this operational impasse requires re-engineering MRV workflows as automated, software-driven digital pipelines (dMRV). Cloud-native geospatial computation platforms and public Earth observation streams provide the raw analytical bandwidth necessary to monitor terrestrial changes at zero marginal per-holding query expense [4], [5]. However, translating raw observation matrices into legally compliant, tamper-resistant carbon assets introduces rigorous software engineering requirements:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Developing low-latency asynchronous backends capable of orchestrating geospatial extraction and machine learning inference on demand;
  \item Designing robust stacking regression topologies that generalize across heterogeneous soil types without overfitting localized samples;
  \item Constructing lightweight, fraud-resistant cryptographic provenance mechanisms operable over low-bandwidth rural mobile networks without the computational tax of proof-of-work distributed ledgers.
\end{enumerate}

To address these requirements, we present the systems design, algorithmic implementation, and empirical validation of \textbf{Krishi-Drishti}. The core engineering contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A distributed microservice architecture pairing a Capacitor-based cross-platform mobile client with an asynchronous FastAPI backend and Google Earth Engine analytics;
  \item A two-tier stacked ensemble machine learning engine integrating bagging (RF) and gradient boosting (XGBoost, CatBoost) with an $L_2$-regularized meta-estimator, achieving $R^2 = 0.89$ and RMSE $= 1.52\,$g/kg on 50 field validation plots;
  \item An algorithmic Four-Pillar readiness gate that evaluates cadastral coordinates, baseline documentation, orbital telemetry, and geotagged records to compress audit cycles from months to under 48 hours;
  \item A sequential SHA-256 audit ledger producing cryptographically verifiable HarvestTokens anchored by diurnal Merkle roots;
  \item An edge-optimized mobile convolutional vision module (MobileNetV2, 88.1\% accuracy) delivering disease diagnostics while restricting remediations to organic protocols to safeguard carbon eligibility.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Works}

\subsection{Carbon Accounting Protocols and Verification Systems}
Regulatory standard-setting organizations have formalized rigorous criteria for voluntary and compliance carbon assets. Gokul et al. [1] analyzed India's domestic Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS), noting that automated digital verification is indispensable if agricultural sectors are to participate meaningfully. Internationally, Verra's VM0042 methodology defines strict protocols for soil organic carbon estimation and additionality enforcement [3]. Abiri et al. [2] reviewed the socio-economic barriers facing digital carbon adoption, emphasizing that high upfront verification expenses prevent inclusive market access. Bhatia [6] investigated AI-driven decision models for Indian smallholders but omitted carbon accounting and monetization pipelines. Krishi-Drishti operationalizes these regulatory requirements into automated computational gates, linking algorithmic compliance directly with cryptographic tokenization.

\subsection{Cloud-Edge Computing in Agricultural Informatics}
Modern agricultural decision-support infrastructures increasingly deploy hybrid cloud architectures to bridge rural network latencies. Thilakarathne et al. [7] implemented cloud-assisted predictive frameworks for dynamic crop recommendation, demonstrating significant utility in regional planning. Nawaz and Babar [8] examined edge-AI computing constraints in resource-limited rural environments, highlighting memory and bandwidth limitations as primary failure modes. In broad IoT settings, Saha et al. [9] demonstrated that multi-sensor field nodes achieve 97.3\% recognition accuracy across automated irrigation and mapping tasks. However, physical sensor grids demand capital investment and continuous on-ground maintenance that exceed smallholder capacity. Krishi-Drishti circumvents physical sensor reliance by shifting data acquisition entirely to public satellite constellations, utilizing mobile devices merely as graphical endpoints and photographic capture nodes.

\subsection{Ensemble Machine Learning for Complex Regressions}
Tabular environmental modeling frequently encounters non-linear feature interactions and localized data sparsity. Khaddi et al. [5] conducted extensive comparisons across machine learning topologies for soil organic carbon modeling, concluding that ensemble techniques consistently outshine individual estimators. Triantakonstantis and Karakostas [10] validated stacked generalization architectures for soil prediction, illustrating that combining tree-based regressors with linear meta-learners mitigates idiosyncratic model biases. Furthermore, Iswarya and Tamizhazhagan [11] demonstrated that quantile random forests yield effective regional soil mapping in Indian agricultural zones, whereas Xiao et al. [12] utilized support vector machines paired with extensive environmental covariates. Our work adopts the stacked generalization paradigm of [10], formulating a tailored ensemble optimized for 12-dimensional spectral feature spaces queryable at runtime.

\subsection{Foliar Computer Vision and Ecological Bioacoustics}
Deep learning architectures for foliar pathology recognition have attained remarkable precision under laboratory evaluation. Iftikhar et al. [13] developed fine-tuned convolutional networks reaching 98.1\% accuracy, Hasan et al. [14] designed lightweight architectures for rice pathology at 97.9\%, and Askale et al. [15] deployed deep networks onto mobile devices for maize leaf classification. Nevertheless, standard agricultural diagnostics operate independently of environmental compliance frameworks, routinely recommending chemical treatments that violate carbon offset additionality and soil conservation guidelines. Complementing visual modalities, non-invasive bioacoustic monitoring has emerged for automated pest detection. Foundational works by Kershenbaum et al. [16] and Hearon et al. [17] demonstrated acoustic feature extraction and soundscape indices for biodiversity surveillance. However, existing bioacoustic tools operate strictly as academic ecological sensors without integration into agricultural MRV pipelines. Krishi-Drishti integrates on-device MobileNetV2 vision and embedded bioacoustic telemetry with language-model policy guards, ensuring diagnostic guidance and biodiversity metrics align with regenerative farming certification.

\subsection{Research Gap}
Taken together, the reviewed literature demonstrates significant progress across remote sensing [4], [5], crop pathology diagnosis [13]--[15], and cryptographic audit mechanisms. However, virtually no reported systems integrate satellite multispectral soil organic carbon regression, Four-Pillar compliance gates, on-device disease diagnostics, and automated carbon tokenization into a unified, cost-effective digital pipeline accessible to smallholders farming sub-two-hectare properties. Krishi-Drishti resolves this fundamental divide.

\section{Proposed Methodology}

\subsection{Stacked Ensemble Regression Engine}
Predicting topsoil carbon concentrations from multispectral inputs requires an estimator that reconciles disparate variance profiles across spectral bands. We implement a stacked generalization model structured into two distinct stages:

\textbf{Level-0 Base Learners:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Random Forest Regressor:} Configured with 200 estimators, max tree depth 15, and bootstrap subsampling, providing variance stabilization across spectral anomalies.
  \item \emph{XGBoost Regressor:} Configured with 300 boosting stages, learning rate 0.05, max depth 6, and column subsample ratio 0.8, minimizing structural residuals.
  \item \emph{CatBoost Regressor:} Implemented with symmetric tree construction and ordered target statistics, preventing overfitting on localized soil groupings.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Level-1 Meta-Learner:}
Out-of-fold predictions generated via 5-fold cross-validation form a $50 \times 3$ meta-feature matrix $\mathbf{Z}$. An $L_2$-regularized Ridge Regression estimator optimizes blending weights:
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\mathbf{w}} \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{Z}\mathbf{w}\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2
  \label{eq:ridge}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda = 1.0$, preventing over-reliance on any single base model while capturing synergistic predictive boundaries.

\subsection{Algorithmic Additionality and Regulatory Gates}
To satisfy Verra VM0042 and CCTS additionality protocols [1], [3], the engine enforces an automated boolean filter. For a farm located in administrative district $d$ practicing agronomic technique $p$, eligibility is governed by:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Eligible}(d, p) = \mathbf{1}\bigl[\Phi(d, p) < 0.50\bigr]
  \label{eq:add_filter}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi(d, p)$ denotes the regional adoption penetration index retrieved from governmental agricultural databases. Practices with adoption exceeding 50\% are rejected at enrollment, ensuring additionality compliance prior to computational processing. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pillars}, candidate projects must clear four sequential evidence gates.

\subsection{Carbon Credit Issuance Engine and Volumetric Stock Inversion}
Translating inverted SOC concentrations into tradeable carbon credit issuances requires rigorous compliance with IPCC Tier-2 and Verra VM0042 standards [1], [3]. Volumetric soil carbon density ($\text{tC/ha}$) is determined by:
\begin{equation}
  \text{SOC}_{\text{stock}} = \left(\frac{\text{SOC}_{\%}}{100}\right) \times \rho_b \times D \times 10
  \label{eq:soc_stock}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_b$ is dry bulk density ($\text{g/cm}^3$) and $D$ represents active profile depth ($0.30\,$m). Net eligible greenhouse gas emission reductions ($\Delta\text{CO}_2\text{e}$) across farm surface area $\mathcal{A}$ are computed as:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  \text{Net}\,\text{tCO}_2\text{e} &= (\text{SOC}_{\text{stock},\,t} - \text{SOC}_{\text{stock},\,t-1}) \times \frac{44}{12} \\
  &\quad \times \mathcal{A} \times (1 - \delta_{\text{leak}}) \times (1 - \delta_{\text{buf}})
\end{split}
\label{eq:net_credits}
\end{equation}
where the stoichiometric factor $44/12$ converts elemental carbon to molecular $\text{CO}_2$, $\delta_{\text{leak}} = 0.10$ denotes the statutory leakage discount, and $\delta_{\text{buf}} = 0.15$ accounts for the permanence risk buffer contribution. Every verified metric tonne of $\text{CO}_2\text{e}$ is issued as a tradeable HarvestToken, triggering an automated payout of 80\% of credit value (\rupee 1,200/$\text{tCO}_2\text{e}$) directly into the cultivator's integrated digital wallet. Fig.~\ref{fig:lifecycle} outlines this eight-stage lifecycle.

\subsection{Cryptographic HarvestToken Ledger}
To provide verifiable provenance without the latency and transaction costs of public blockchain networks, each approved credit is issued into a cryptographic ledger. Each token contains a sequential SHA-256 state digest:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{T}_i = \text{SHA-256}\bigl(\text{UUID}_i \mathbin{\|} \text{GPS}_i \mathbin{\|} \text{MetricVector}_i \mathbin{\|} \Delta\text{CO}_2 \mathbin{\|} \mathcal{T}_{i-1}\bigr)
  \label{eq:ledger_hash}
\end{equation}
At 00:00 UTC daily, all newly minted tokens are compiled into a binary Merkle tree, generating a 32-byte Merkle Root Anchor that seals the daily epoch against retroactive manipulation.

As summarized in Table~\ref{tab:comp}, recent literature offers compelling point solutions for agriculture, yet each exhibits critical operational limitations that Krishi-Drishti directly overcomes:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Crop Pathology Isolation:} Visual diagnostic systems such as Iftikhar et al. [13], Hasan et al. [14], and Askale et al. [15] obtain high classification accuracy (97.9\%--98.1\%), but operate as isolated endpoints without carbon accounting or monetization links. Crucially, conventional models often recommend synthetic fungicides or chemical pesticides that disqualify smallholders from carbon offset programs. Krishi-Drishti overcomes this gap by integrating MobileNetV2 with an LLM advisory layer strictly constrained to organic, carbon-compliant remedies.
  \item \emph{Hardware-Dependent Sensor Grids:} Physical IoT platforms such as Saha et al. [9], Nawaz and Babar [8], and Thilakarathne et al. [7] achieve high precision but require dedicated on-farm sensory hardware and power infrastructure. These deployment costs are prohibitive for marginal cultivators with sub-two-hectare plots. Krishi-Drishti overcomes this physical dependency by shifting environmental sensing entirely to public orbital Sentinel-2 imagery [4], [5], requiring zero field hardware beyond the farmer's smartphone.
  \item \emph{Absence of End-to-End MRV Pipelines:} Spatial SOC modeling studies, including the quantile random forests of Iswarya and Tamizhazhagan [11], the stacking models of Triantakonstantis and Karakostas [10], and the support vector regression of Xiao et al. [12], focus strictly on cartographic estimation. None construct a complete verification and crediting pipeline. Similarly, while Bhatia [6] provided AI-driven agricultural decision models and Abiri et al. [2] analyzed economic barriers, neither operationalized verifiable carbon issuance. Krishi-Drishti overcomes these limitations by integrating spectral SOC estimation with automated additionality screening, Four-Pillar verification, and SHA-256 HarvestToken minting aligned with Verra VM0042 and CCTS frameworks [1], [3].
  \item \emph{Ecological Telemetry Integration:} Standalone bioacoustic systems such as Kershenbaum et al. [16] and Hearon et al. [17] operate strictly as biodiversity monitoring tools. Krishi-Drishti incorporates bioacoustic monitoring as an integrated microservice feeding directly into the carbon project registry to corroborate co-benefit claims.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion and Future Works}
This research demonstrates that coupling cloud-native geospatial microservices with stacked ensemble machine learning and cryptographic audit chaining provides a scalable software solution to smallholder carbon verification. By achieving $R^2 = 0.89$ and RMSE $= 1.52\,$g/kg in topsoil carbon inversion, Krishi-Drishti eliminates dependency on cost-prohibitive physical diagnostic laboratories. The automated Four-Pillar compliance pipeline shortens verification cycles from over six months to under 48 hours, while sequential SHA-256 state digests guarantee immutable auditability without the latency penalties of distributed consensus networks. Future research will explore federated edge learning for on-device model personalization, Sentinel-1 radar fusion during monsoon cloud covers, and multi-spectral calibration across diverse soil taxonomies.
